# Visual design

The slides support a live speaker. Give each slide one visual question, reveal, or takeaway. Put derivations, caveats, and extended explanations in speaker notes. Use a comic beat or pop-culture reference when it reinforces the concept and works for students unfamiliar with the reference.

## Canvas and typography

- Design for a 16:9 canvas and responsive scaling. Keep labels and speech bubbles inside safe margins, with room for presenter controls and browser or projector cropping.
- Use an off-white paper background, near-black ink, and a restrained accent palette. Pair color with labels, line patterns, shapes, or position when encoding states such as a dropped message or a conflicting vote.
- Use legible handwritten typography for short text and a readable supporting face where appropriate. Select and license locally bundled fonts in M1. Establish size and stroke-width targets through projected trials.
- Favor a few large labels, clear reading order, and generous whitespace. Keep equations sparse and large. Verify critical text and linework on a classroom projector from typical audience distances.

## Illustration and motion

- Draw original stick figures with readable silhouettes, simple poses, expressive faces, and concise speech bubbles. Take inspiration from the general educational explainer aesthetic of MinuteEarth, MinutePhysics, and XKCD. Create original poses, characters, compositions, and jokes.
- Prefer reusable SVG character parts and props, with stable Rough.js seeds for imperfect ink strokes. Preserve stable visual roles within a scene, especially for senders, recipients, validators, and adversarial actors.
- Use movement to show a meaningful change such as message delivery, a split history, or a computation step. Pause at states worth discussing. Give interactive demonstrations one clear task and a visible reset.
- Use fictional names, transactions, and scenarios. Optional pop-culture references should use original imagery. Keep toy cryptography labels visible wherever students operate the demonstration.
- Plan a print composition for each slide. Comics can use their full panel sequence. Animated and interactive scenes need an informative still or compact sequence that preserves the teaching point.
